Our position

Why VettID

VettID exists to help ordinary people get security, privacy, and trust online — without needing to become experts.

The internet asks you to trade your privacy for participation. Every account, every app, every "I agree" hands over a little more of you to someone whose business depends on knowing you better than your friends do. We think that trade is broken. We're building the proof.

Where we stand

Most companies have a mission statement. We have a position, and we'd rather you know it up front.

"I have nothing to hide"

You've heard it. Maybe you've said it. If you're not doing anything wrong, why worry about privacy?

Here's the problem with that line: it gets privacy exactly backwards. Privacy isn't a shield for wrongdoing — it's a right that belongs to people doing nothing wrong at all. You close the bathroom door. You seal the envelope. You don't read your neighbor's mail. None of that is hiding; it's dignity, and nobody asks you to justify it.

We'd take it a step further, because this belief is the foundation everything we build stands on:

A strong, positive assertion of your identity entitles you to your right to privacy.

Read that again, because it inverts how the internet works today. Online, you're constantly asked to prove who you are by surrendering information — your email, your phone number, your birthday, your browsing habits, whatever the form demands. The more of yourself you give away, the more "trusted" you become, and the less privacy you have left. We think it should work the other way: when you can prove exactly who you are — no more, no less, on your terms — you've earned the right to keep everything else to yourself. Being known and being safe should not be opposites.

Identity and anonymity are different tools, not enemies

Some of the people we respect most in privacy hold anonymity as a core value. We want to be precise about where we agree and where we differ.

Anonymity is the right tool against powerful adversaries. Political dissidents, investigative journalists, abuse survivors — people whose safety depends on not being identified need anonymity, and we defend it without reservation. A world without anonymity is a world where speaking truth to power gets you found.

But anonymity is the wrong tool for building trusted relationships. Your bank, your doctor, your kid's school — these relationships require knowing who's on the other end. And sometimes the stakes are closer to home: when the phone rings and a familiar voice says "Grandma, I'm in trouble, I need money" — is that actually your grandchild? Scammers are already using AI to clone the voices of people you love, and they're counting on one thing: that you have no way to check. Anonymity doesn't protect you in that moment. What protects you is the ability to verify — to know, not hope, that the person on the other end is who they claim to be. Trust needs accountability, and accountability needs identity. A society that runs on anonymity alone doesn't get more freedom; it hands its infrastructure to whoever is most comfortable being unaccountable.

Here's the tragedy: the internet we actually got forces everyone into a third thing that is worse than either. Call it surveilled pseudo-identity. You're anonymous enough that no one truly vouches for you — scammers thrive, trust is impossible — yet surveilled enough that everything you do is collected, correlated, and sold. You get none of anonymity's protection and none of identity's trust. Everyone loses except the people doing the collecting.

The answer isn't less privacy, and it isn't mandatory anonymity. It's identity architecture where you control what is asserted, to whom, and nothing more.

How this shapes what we build

A position is cheap. Here's how ours shows up in VettID itself — so you can check our claims against our work.

You hold your keys. Not us.

Your credentials live in a vault that only you can unlock. There's no recovery department that can peek inside, no master key in a drawer somewhere, no employee who could be bribed or subpoenaed into handing over your data. This isn't a policy we promise to follow — it's an architecture that removes the choice. We couldn't access your vault if we wanted to, and we designed it that way on purpose.

There is no door to knock on.

Most systems protect your data by guarding the doors. We built a vault with no inbound path at all — nothing on the internet can reach into it, because the architecture doesn't have a way in. Your vault reaches out on your instruction; nothing reaches in. Guarding doors means hoping the guard never fails. Having no door means there's nothing to fail.

Prove exactly what's needed, and nothing more.

When you assert your identity through VettID, you decide what gets shared, with whom, for how long. Proving you're over 21 shouldn't require handing over your birthdate, address, and a photo of your license. Strong identity and minimal disclosure aren't opposites — done right, they're the same feature.

Verification runs both ways.

Scams work because verification is one-sided: they can demand you prove yourself, but you can't check them back. VettID connections are mutual — both ends prove who they are, every time, automatically. That grandmother we mentioned? In a VettID world, "is this really my grandchild?" has a cryptographic answer, not a hopeful guess.

One honest sentence about all of this.

Our worldview shapes what we build and which problems we choose to solve. You don't have to share it to check our work — everything above is architecture, not a promise, and we'd rather earn your trust with work you can verify than with words you have to take on faith. The technical version lives in our code and the rest of vettid.org.
